# Population Variation: AuDHD, Women and Hormones, Late-Diagnosed, Students, Aging

## TL;DR

- The rest of this corpus, by necessity, designs for a **monolithic ADHD adult**. That persona is a useful center of mass, but at least five sub-populations with large footprints flip *specific* defaults in *opposite* directions. The fix is almost never a rebuild — it is **presets and toggles with safe defaults**, plus a discipline of never letting one group's optimization become another's harm.
- **AuDHD** (co-occurring autism and ADHD) is the sharpest internal contradiction in the whole corpus. Autism co-occurs in roughly **14% of children with ADHD**, and about **38–40% of autistic people also have ADHD** (see [adhd-overview.md](adhd-overview.md)). The ADHD-optimized mechanics elsewhere in this corpus — novelty rotation, surprise celebrations, animated rewards, sound/haptics — are exactly the ones that can *hurt* the autistic side (sensory overload, need for sameness).
- **Monotropism** (Murray, Lawson & Lesser) — the model of autistic attention as a narrow, deep "tunnel" — explains why many AuDHD users need change to be *announced, not sprung*, and why "surprise me" is a threat, not a treat, for a large minority. Literal-language copy (no idioms, sarcasm, or ambiguous microcopy) is a cheap win that helps them and harms no one.
- **Women and cycling hormones**: estrogen modulates dopamine, and symptom worsening in the low-estrogen **luteal phase** is clinically credible but genuinely under-researched — ADDitude's own experts say "there is almost no research validating this relationship" [2]. Survey figures collected in a 2025 review: ~66% of women with ADHD report PMS/PMDD; premenstrual loss of focus ~66% [1].
- **Perimenopause and menopause** bring large self-reported spikes (a survey found 70% rating ADHD "life-altering" in their 40s–50s; 50% "extremely severe" in perimenopause) [1]. Menopausal "brain fog" is routinely **confused with new-onset ADHD in one direction and with dementia in the other** — a diagnostic trap that shapes who arrives at Klyr and why.
- **Cycle-aware planning** can responsibly offer *optional*, user-labeled lighter luteal-phase targets and gentler copy — but must **never auto-infer a cycle from behavior**, never require period logging to function, and always label the science as emerging (the consistency angle is owned by [habits-and-routines.md](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md)).
- **Late-diagnosed adults** are ~**56% of current US adult diagnoses** [adhd-overview.md]. Diagnosis reliably triggers **relief *and* grief** at once: in one study of late-diagnosed women (n=28), 93% grieved "what could have been," 96% reported negative self-perception *before* diagnosis, and 25% used explicit language of being "cheated" or "failed" [3]. Onboarding tuned for a bright, fresh productivity app lands badly on someone carrying decades of internalized failure.
- **Students and teens** live inside **externally-imposed deadline ecosystems** (grades, terms, parents, teachers) rather than the self-authored task lists adults manage; they may have legal **accommodations** (504 plan / IEP / ADA) Klyr can support; and they raise hard **parental-visibility ethics** (support vs. surveillance). **COPPA** hard-gates data collection from under-13s behind verifiable parental consent [5].
- **Older adults**: normal age-related executive-function decline *stacks on top of* ADHD; digital fluency is lower in some cohorts; and the menopause/ADHD/mild-cognitive-impairment confusion peaks here. Do not assume the young power-user with a widget-covered home screen.
- **The one near-universal**: forgiveness, low shame, and near-zero-cost capture serve every group. The deltas are mostly about **sensory intensity, novelty, time pressure, and disclosure** — and every one of those is better shipped as a preset than baked into a single house style.

## Why this doc exists

Every other doc in this corpus makes a defensible simplification: it designs for one ADHD adult. But "person with ADHD" is not a uniform target. The same feature that rescues one user actively harms another, and the split usually falls along **membership in a sub-population**, not personal taste. This doc maps the five sub-populations whose needs diverge most from the corpus default, states the mechanism behind each divergence, and ends with a **population-by-design-delta matrix**: which of Klyr's defaults are safe for everyone, which must become presets, and which single features help one group while harming another. It deliberately goes *deeper* on tensions the other docs only flag, and cross-links rather than restates their content.

## 1. AuDHD: the novelty-versus-sameness collision

**AuDHD** is the community shorthand for co-occurring autism and ADHD. It is common: autism co-occurs in roughly 14% of children with ADHD, and pooled estimates put ADHD at ~38.5% (current) / 40.2% (lifetime) within autistic populations — see the comorbidity table in [adhd-overview.md](adhd-overview.md). For a large share of Klyr's users, "ADHD design" and "autism design" are pulling on the same person at the same time, in opposite directions.

**The core tension.** Most ADHD-optimized engagement leans on *novelty, surprise, stimulation, and change* — the levers in [motivation-and-gamification.md](../strategies/motivation-and-gamification.md) and the novelty-decay logic in [habits-and-routines.md](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md). Autism, by contrast, is characterized by a preference for **predictability, routine, and sensory regulation**, and by distress when those are violated. So the very mechanics that keep the ADHD side engaged — rotating themes, "surprise me" suggestions, confetti bursts, sound and haptic flourishes, layout that reshuffles to stay fresh — are the mechanics most likely to *dysregulate* the autistic side.

**Monotropism** gives this a mechanism. Proposed by Dinah Murray, Wenn Lawson, and Mike Lesser (foundational paper 2005; term coined by Murray in the early 1990s), monotropism models autistic cognition as a tendency to channel attention into a **small number of deep "attention tunnels"** that consume most of the available attention at once [4]. Interests, once active, are hard to leave; shifting attention out of a tunnel is *costly*, which is why unexpected interruptions and changes of plan can feel not merely annoying but genuinely painful. Monotropism has been posited as a central underlying feature of autism, and it also names something ADHDers recognize as **hyperfocus** — the same deep-tunnel state, differently framed (see [attention-and-hyperfocus.md](attention-and-hyperfocus.md)). *Note on evidence: monotropism is a well-regarded theory and increasingly studied, but it is a model, not settled fact; the Wikipedia summary consulted here does not itself equate monotropism with ADHD hyperfocus — that overlap is a reasonable synthesis, not an established finding [4].*

Design consequences that follow directly:

- **Change should be announced, not sprung.** For the AuDHD user, a UI that silently reorganizes to "stay fresh" is a betrayal of the predictability their autistic side depends on. Any novelty Klyr introduces (new theme, moved button, seasonal event) should be *opt-in* or at minimum previewed, never ambient.
- **Sensory output must be dial-able to near-zero.** Animated rewards, celebration sound, haptic buzzes, color intensity, and motion are stimulating for one profile and aversive for another. This is not a fringe accommodation: `prefers-reduced-motion` is already a mandate in [ux-design-for-adhd.md](../product/ux-design-for-adhd.md); AuDHD extends it to sound, haptics, and color saturation as first-class user controls.
- **"Surprise me" must be a feature the user reaches for, never a default behavior.** Variable/surprise rewards are one of the strongest ADHD engagement levers and one of the clearest autistic stressors. Ship it as an explicit, reversible choice.

**Literal-language copy.** Autistic users disproportionately parse language literally; idioms, sarcasm, playful ambiguity, and "cute" microcopy that hides the actual action ("Let's do this!" on a button whose function is unclear) impose real comprehension cost. Plain, literal, unambiguous copy — say exactly what a control does — helps the autistic side and costs the ADHD side nothing; it also aligns with the cognitive-load minimization already argued in [ux-design-for-adhd.md](../product/ux-design-for-adhd.md). This is a rare win with no trade-off, so it should be **house style, not a preset**.

**PDA overlap.** Pathological/pervasive demand avoidance — the flip from "want" to "can't" the instant something becomes an expectation — sits at the AuDHD intersection and is common in this group. The general phenomenon and its contested clinical status are owned by [motivation-and-gamification.md](../strategies/motivation-and-gamification.md); the population note here is only that **demand-sensitivity intensity clusters in AuDHD users**, so the autonomy-preserving, low-coercion patterns that doc recommends matter *most* for exactly the users who most need Klyr's other autistic accommodations. Autonomy-supportive framing and sensory calm are not separate features for this group — they travel together.

## 2. Women and cycling hormones

Sex-ratio and late-diagnosis basics (masking, inattentive presentation, help-seeking that lands on anxiety/depression first) are covered in [adhd-overview.md](adhd-overview.md). This section goes deeper on the part with direct product implications: **within-person symptom variation driven by hormones**, which turns "consistency" into a moving biological target rather than a willpower question.

**The mechanism.** Estrogen modulates dopaminergic function; when estrogen is low or declining in a brain where dopamine signaling is already atypical, the two "shortages" reinforce each other [1][2]. This is the best current explanation for a pattern women with ADHD report consistently:

- **Follicular phase** (roughly days 1–14, estrogen rising/high): for many with an inattentive, low-energy profile, this is the "good" stretch — more energy, clearer focus, better mood. Paradoxically, for a hyperactive-impulsive profile, high-estrogen states can be *more* challenging [2].
- **Luteal phase** (premenstrual, estrogen falling/low): more depression, anxiety, stress, sleep disruption, binge eating, and cognitive/memory difficulty — the ADHD symptom floor drops just as PMS arrives [2].

**The numbers, honestly graded.** A 2025 review of female ADHD aggregates survey and self-report data: about **66% of women with ADHD reported PMS/PMDD**; premenstrual symptoms included irritability (80%), mood swings (79%), tension/anxiety (68%), and lack of focus/concentration (66%); and a first self-report study (n=209) found premenstrual depressive symptoms in **45% of women with ADHD vs 28% of the general population** [1]. These are mostly cross-sectional, self-reported figures, and the review is explicit about it: research on female ADHD "remains limited, as most studies have been male-dominated," and "we currently have little information on pharmacokinetics under the influence of hormonal fluctuations" [1]. ADDitude's clinical writers put it even more bluntly: "there is almost no research validating this relationship," and their guidance derives from "anecdotal information and clinical observations" [2]. **Status: clinically credible, mechanistically plausible, evidence thin — treat as emerging.**

**Stimulant efficacy may vary across the cycle**, which matters because a user's baseline capability (and therefore what Klyr can reasonably expect of them) can shift with medication that is working better or worse depending on where they are in the month. Estrogen and dopamine "potentiate each other," so a fixed dose can feel too strong in a high-estrogen phase and insufficient in the luteal phase; "cycle dosing" is discussed clinically but not established [2]. The stimulant-efficacy detail is owned by [habits-and-routines.md](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md); the population implication here is that **a woman's "normal" is not one setpoint but an orbit**, and a system that scores consistency against a single line will mislabel a predictable hormonal dip as personal failure.

**Perimenopause and menopause** deserve their own beat because the estrogen decline is sustained, not cyclical. In the same review's survey data, **70% of women rated ADHD's impact "life-altering" in their 40s–50s** and **50% described it as "extremely severe" during perimenopause**, with the most debilitating problems being procrastination/time management (79%), working memory (74%), overwhelm (72%), and disorganization (70%) [1]. Small trials found that stimulants and atomoxetine improved executive-function difficulties in peri- and post-menopausal women *without* an ADHD diagnosis — which is the diagnostic-confusion problem in one sentence: menopausal cognitive change and ADHD look alike, and the review notes that **no study has specifically examined executive-function decline in women with ADHD across the menopausal transition** [1]. (Pregnancy and postpartum add their own layer — a self-reported postpartum-depression rate of 61% and 5–6× elevated risk in one dataset, plus near-universal stimulant discontinuation in pregnancy [1] — but that is a life-stage support question more than a core planning-UI question, so it is flagged rather than developed here.)

**What cycle-aware planning can responsibly offer.** The temptation is to build a smart "hormone mode" that predicts bad days. Resist the aggressive version. A defensible feature set:

- **Optional, opt-in, and explicitly labeled emerging.** A user can turn on lighter luteal-phase expectations (reduced targets, gentler copy, a pre-warned "this week may be harder — that's biology, not you"). It ships off by default and says outright that the science is preliminary.
- **User-declared, never inferred.** Klyr must never *deduce* a menstrual cycle from usage patterns or lateness. That is both scientifically unreliable and a privacy landmine.
- **Period logging is never a prerequisite.** The mode must add value from a simple user-set marker; it must not force intimate health tracking into a task app, and any such data must be treated as sensitive.

## 3. Late-diagnosed adults

About **56% of current US adult ADHD diagnoses were made in adulthood** ([adhd-overview.md](adhd-overview.md)). This is not a minor segment; it may be the *modal* new Klyr user. And they arrive carrying something the corpus default does not model: **decades of internalized failure that a diagnosis has just re-labeled.**

The emotional signature is specific and well-documented. A mixed-methods study of late-diagnosed women (n=28; diagnosed at a mean age of 35.9, range 18–62) found a consistent two-sided response [3]:

- **Before diagnosis, near-universal self-blame.** 96% described negative self-perception; 68% could not understand why they struggled differently from peers. One participant: *"It felt like everyone was playing a game I didn't get the instructions to."* 89% reported being dismissed — often by professionals who attributed symptoms to anxiety or depression, or doubted the ADHD because the person was outwardly successful [3].
- **After diagnosis, relief *and* grief in the same breath.** 86% reported improved self-perception and 57% described active "healing" — but 93% also reflected on "what could have been," and 25% used explicit loss language: *cheated, failed, missed out.* One woman diagnosed at 48: *"That's forty years of living with guilt and shame about who I was… It all could have been avoided."* [3]

The sample is small and self-selected, and it is women specifically — but it converges tightly with the broader late-diagnosis literature and community accounts referenced in [emotional-regulation-and-rsd.md](emotional-regulation-and-rsd.md), and the pattern (relief-plus-grief, identity reconstruction, retrospective mourning) is robust enough to design around.

**Onboarding-tone implications.** A default onboarding written for a fresh, optimistic productivity user — "Let's build better habits! You've got this!" — reads very differently to someone who just learned that thirty years of "you're smart, you're just not trying" had a name the whole time. Concretely:

- **Name the reframe, don't chirp past it.** Copy that acknowledges "your past struggles were not a character flaw" does real work for this user; relentless positivity can feel like it is talking to someone else.
- **Never imply the user is starting from zero or has been failing at self-management.** They know. The framing should be "here is scaffolding your brain never had," not "here is how to finally get organized."
- **Do not require a confident self-model on day one.** A newly-diagnosed user often does not yet know their own patterns; onboarding that demands they declare their priorities, energy peaks, and goals up front asks for knowledge they are still assembling. Harvest configuration from use (already Klyr's stance in [ux-design-for-adhd.md](../product/ux-design-for-adhd.md)) — it matters doubly here.
- **Support disclosure of a *fluid* identity.** Some users are newly, tentatively identifying with ADHD; some are undiagnosed and unsure; the product should never gate value behind a formal diagnosis or imply that self-identifiers are illegitimate.

## 4. Students and teens

Students are not just younger adults with the same task list. Their **task ecosystem is externally imposed and socially visible** in a way most adult work is not, and they sit inside legal and ethical structures adults do not.

**Deadline ecosystems.** A student's obligations arrive from teachers, syllabi, and terms on someone else's schedule, often with high stakes (grades, GPA, college admission) and long, opaque lead times — the exact structure that ADHD time-discounting handles worst (see [time-perception.md](time-perception.md)). A task app for students has to ingest and represent *externally-owned* deadlines and multi-week projects with distant due dates, not just self-authored to-dos. The design pressure is toward **decomposition and runway** (breaking a three-week essay into today-sized pieces) more than toward capture alone.

**Accommodations.** Many students with ADHD have formal accommodations under one of three US frameworks (equivalents exist elsewhere): a **504 plan** (adjustments like extended time, reduced distraction, extended deadlines under civil-rights law), an **IEP** (a special-education plan with services and goals), or, in higher ed and beyond, the **ADA**. *Evidence note: this legal structure is well-established US special-education/disability law but was not re-verified against a primary source in this research pass — teams building student features should confirm current specifics.* The product opportunity is to let a student encode "I get 1.5× time" or "extended deadline" as a first-class property, and to reflect accommodations in how due dates and reminders are computed rather than making the student do that math under pressure.

**Parental-visibility ethics.** This is the genuinely hard one. Parental involvement can be scaffolding *or* surveillance, and the same feature (a parent dashboard, shared task visibility) can be either depending on consent and framing. The research-backed risk is that ADHD kids and teens already receive an outsized volume of correction and control (the "controlled motivation" problem in [motivation-and-gamification.md](../strategies/motivation-and-gamification.md)), and a product that hands parents a real-time compliance monitor can deepen exactly the autonomy deficit that undermines long-term motivation. Design stance: any cross-account visibility should be **consented-to by the teen, scoped, and non-punitive by construction** (share *that a plan exists and progress is happening*, not a granular feed of every miss), and the teen should know precisely what is visible. Defaulting to full parental transparency is a dark pattern wearing a helpful hat.

**COPPA and age gating.** In the US, the **Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)** applies to services directed to **children under 13**, or with actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information from an under-13 user, and requires **verifiable parental consent** before collecting that data [5]. *(The rule was amended in 2025; the specific changes were not verified in this pass and should be checked against current FTC text.)* Practical consequence for Klyr: serving under-13s pulls in a heavy consent-and-data-minimization regime, so the cleanest initial posture is to **target 13+**, gate younger users behind a parent-managed flow if ever supported, and keep data collection minimal regardless of age. Teens (13–17) are outside COPPA but still warrant conservative data practices and the visibility ethics above.

## 5. Older adults

ADHD does not end at midlife. Symptoms persist in roughly 71% of childhood cases even where full diagnostic criteria are no longer met ([adhd-overview.md](adhd-overview.md)), and a growing cohort is diagnosed *for the first time* in their 50s, 60s, and beyond. Older adults are underrepresented in ADHD research and in most ADHD apps' implicit persona; three things distinguish them.

**Executive-function decline stacks on ADHD.** Normal aging slows processing speed and erodes working memory even in neurotypical people; layered onto a lifelong executive-function difference, the *combined* load can be heavier than either alone. *Evidence note: the additive framing here is a reasonable mechanistic inference and consistent with the aging and ADHD literatures generally, but was not verified against a specific source in this pass.* Practically, the externalization Klyr already champions (offloading memory and sequencing into the tool — see [executive-function.md](executive-function.md)) becomes *more* load-bearing with age, not less; features that assume a fast, capacious working memory to hold context between steps will strain this group first.

**Menopause / ADHD / cognitive-aging confusion.** As covered in §2, menopausal cognitive change mimics ADHD, and both mimic early mild-cognitive-impairment worries. The result is a population arriving at Klyr with real uncertainty about *what* they have — newly-diagnosed ADHD, hormonal change, ordinary aging, or something to see a doctor about. Klyr's job is emphatically **not** to adjudicate this; the product stance is the same non-diagnostic, non-alarming scaffolding it offers everyone, plus a hard rule to **never editorialize about cognitive decline** ("forgetting again?" copy is corrosive here in a specific, frightening way).

**Tech comfort varies more.** Digital fluency is, on average, lower and more variable in older cohorts — though this is a shrinking cohort effect, not a fact about age, and plenty of older users are highly capable. The safe design posture: do not assume familiarity with gesture-driven UI, widgets, notification management, or app conventions; make the happy path legible without discovery; and keep text large and adjustable (the typography floor in [ux-design-for-adhd.md](../product/ux-design-for-adhd.md) — which already assumes comorbid dyslexia — serves aging vision too). Avoid designs whose core value is locked behind home-screen widgets or wearables the user may not have.

## 6. Population-by-design-delta matrix

The practical payload. For each design lever, is the corpus default **universal** (ship one way for everyone), a **preset** (varies by declared population/need), or a **collision** (one group's help is another's harm, so it must be a safe-default toggle)?

| Design lever | Corpus default (monolithic adult) | Verdict | Who flips it, and how |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forgiveness / no-shame / cheap capture** | On | **Universal** | Every group needs it; if anything, late-diagnosed and older adults need it *more*. Never make it a preset. |
| **Literal, plain copy** | Should be house style | **Universal** | AuDHD needs it; nobody is harmed by it. Promote from nicety to rule. |
| **Novelty rotation (themes, fresh layouts, "surprise me")** | Renewable engagement resource | **Collision** | ADHD-only: helpful. AuDHD: harmful (sameness need). Ship as opt-in; default to stable. |
| **Animated rewards, celebration sound, haptics** | Small purposeful celebration OK | **Collision** | ADHD-only: motivating. AuDHD / sensory-sensitive: aversive. Independent dials for motion, sound, haptics, color intensity; low or off as the safe default. |
| **Variable / surprise rewards** | Strong ADHD lever (use cautiously) | **Collision** | AuDHD: stressor. Older adults: often unwanted. Opt-in only. |
| **Manufactured urgency / countdowns** | Opt-in tool, not ambient | **Collision** | Motivating for some ADHDers; anxiety-activating for the many with comorbid anxiety, and for late-diagnosed users mid-grief. Keep opt-in (already the corpus stance). |
| **Streaks** | Potent but need forgiveness | **Collision** | Students may respond to them; RSD-prone and shame-carrying users (esp. late-diagnosed) are harmed by loss framing. Forgiving by construction, and skippable. |
| **Onboarding tone** | Value in first minutes | **Preset** | Late-diagnosed: acknowledge the reframe, avoid chirpy "start fresh." New adult self-identifiers: don't gate on diagnosis. Teens: age-appropriate. |
| **Configuration harvested from use, not asked up front** | Yes | **Universal** — extra-load-bearing for **late-diagnosed** (no self-model yet) and **older adults** (lower tech comfort). |
| **Cycle / season-aware expectations** | Emerging, optional | **Preset** | Cycling women: opt-in lighter luteal targets, user-declared, never inferred, no forced period logging. Off for everyone else. |
| **Externally-owned deadlines + long-runway decomposition** | Capture-first | **Preset** | Students/teens: first-class syllabus deadlines and multi-week breakdown. Adults benefit but need it less. |
| **Accommodation encoding (1.5× time, extended deadlines)** | Absent | **Preset** | Students with 504/IEP/ADA plans; niche for others. |
| **Cross-account (parent) visibility** | Absent | **Collision / preset** | Teens: can be scaffolding *or* surveillance. Teen-consented, scoped, non-punitive only; never default-on. |
| **Under-13 data collection** | Not designed for | **Hard constraint** | COPPA: verifiable parental consent required. Cleanest posture is 13+ only initially [5]. |
| **Assumes fast working memory to hold context between steps** | Implicit | **Preset toward the floor** | Older adults and high-EF-load days need more externalization and fewer hold-this-in-your-head transitions. |
| **Large, adjustable typography; legible happy path** | Accessibility floor | **Universal** | Serves dyslexia, aging vision, and cognitive load at once. |

Reading the matrix: the **universals are about respect and load** (forgiveness, plain language, cheap capture, legibility). The **collisions cluster around stimulation and pressure** (novelty, animation, surprise, urgency, streaks, visibility) — which is precisely why Klyr should treat sensory intensity, novelty, and pressure as **user-owned dials with calm defaults**, not as a fixed personality. The **presets are about life-stage and biology** (onboarding tone, cycle awareness, student deadlines, accommodations, working-memory assumptions).

## Design implications for Klyr

1. **Make sensory intensity a first-class settings group with calm defaults.** Independent controls for motion, celebration sound, haptics, and color saturation — defaulting low — because the same output delights the ADHD-only user and dysregulates the AuDHD user, and only the user knows which they are. This extends the existing `prefers-reduced-motion` mandate in [ux-design-for-adhd.md](../product/ux-design-for-adhd.md) to a full sensory panel.
2. **Never make novelty or "surprise" ambient.** Rotating themes, reshuffled layouts, and surprise rewards must be opt-in and, where they change the interface, previewed — because monotropic users experience unannounced change as a real cost, not a delight [4]. Stability is the safe default; novelty is a feature the user reaches for.
3. **Adopt literal, plain copy as house style, not a toggle.** No idioms, sarcasm, or action-hiding cuteness on functional controls; say what the button does. It helps autistic users measurably and harms no one — a rare no-trade-off win.
4. **Ship an optional, opt-in cycle-aware mode that is user-declared and privacy-safe.** Lighter luteal-phase targets and gentler copy, explicitly labeled emerging science; never infer a cycle from behavior, never require period logging, and treat any cycle marker as sensitive health data [1][2]. Coordinate with the consistency-metric logic in [habits-and-routines.md](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md).
5. **Model "normal" as an orbit, not a line, for hormonally cycling and perimenopausal users.** Consistency scoring that compares against a single setpoint will mislabel a predictable, biology-driven dip as personal failure; rolling/forgiving metrics (already argued for streaks) absorb this correctly [1].
6. **Tune onboarding for the late-diagnosed as the likely modal new user (~56% of adult diagnoses).** Acknowledge the reframe ("your past struggles weren't a character flaw"), avoid "start fresh / you've got this" chirpiness, and never imply the user has been failing at self-management [3]. Offer, don't force, a gentle framing of scaffolding-they-never-had.
7. **Never gate value behind a formal diagnosis or a confident self-model.** New self-identifiers and the freshly-diagnosed are still assembling their own patterns; harvest configuration from use and let identity stay fluid — no "confirm your diagnosis" walls, no day-one demand to declare priorities and energy peaks.
8. **Ban cognitive-decline editorializing everywhere, and hard-ban it for older users.** "Forgetting again?" or "you've really slipped" copy is corrosive generally and specifically frightening to older adults navigating the ADHD/menopause/MCI confusion. Klyr is non-diagnostic scaffolding and never comments on the user's cognition.
9. **Give students externally-owned deadlines and long-runway decomposition as first-class features, plus accommodation encoding.** Ingest syllabus/term deadlines, break distant high-stakes projects into today-sized pieces, and let a user encode "1.5× time" or "extended deadline" so the system does the pressure math (see [time-perception.md](time-perception.md)). *(Verify accommodation-law specifics before building.)*
10. **Treat any parent/teen visibility as consented, scoped, and non-punitive — never default-on.** Share that a plan exists and progress is happening, not a granular feed of misses; the teen controls and can see exactly what is shared. A default-transparent parent dashboard is a dark pattern that deepens the autonomy deficit ADHD teens already carry.
11. **Adopt a conservative age posture: target 13+, minimize data for everyone.** COPPA's verifiable-parental-consent regime for under-13s is heavy; the clean initial scope is 13+, with any younger support gated behind a parent-managed flow [5]. *(Re-check the 2025 COPPA amendments before shipping anything youth-facing.)*
12. **Push externalization further for older adults and high-load days.** Reduce hold-this-in-your-head transitions, keep the happy path legible without gesture/widget discovery, and don't lock core value behind home-screen widgets or wearables. Aging vision is already served by the dyslexia-driven typography floor — keep it adjustable.
13. **Resolve every collision the same way: safe-default toggle, never house style.** Where a feature helps one population and harms another (novelty, animation, surprise, urgency, streaks, visibility), the answer is a user-owned dial with a calm default — not a single choice imposed on all. This is the operational core of "design for variation."
14. **Do not require users to self-classify into these populations to get the right defaults.** Presets should be discoverable and nameable ("more calm / less surprise," "I was recently diagnosed," "lighter some weeks"), but the *defaults* must already be safe for the most sensitive group, so a user who never touches settings is never harmed.

## Open questions

- **Does cycle-aware planning actually help, or does surfacing "this will be a hard week" become a self-fulfilling excuse or an anxiety trigger?** Needs testing with cycling ADHD users; the underlying symptom-variation science is itself emerging [1][2].
- **What is the right AuDHD default — calm-first for everyone, or a fast fork in onboarding?** Calm-by-default protects the sensitive group but may under-stimulate ADHD-only users on first run and cost early engagement. Untested trade-off.
- **How do late-diagnosed users react to onboarding that explicitly names grief/relief?** It could feel deeply seen or presumptuous and heavy. Requires message testing across the relief–grief spectrum.
- **Where exactly is the ethical line on teen–parent visibility**, and does a well-designed consented version actually preserve autonomy in practice, or do teens consent under pressure? Needs research with ADHD teens, not just parents.
- **How large is the older-adult ADHD segment likely to be for Klyr, and how much does low digital fluency vs. high EF-scaffolding-need net out?** Under-researched population; personas are thin.
- **Do these populations stack?** A late-diagnosed AuDHD woman in perimenopause is not hypothetical. The matrix treats levers independently; real users combine presets, and interaction effects are untested.
