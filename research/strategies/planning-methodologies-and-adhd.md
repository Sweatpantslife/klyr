# Productivity Systems Stress-Tested Against ADHD: GTD, Bullet Journal, Time Blocking, Kanban

## TL;DR

- Every famous productivity system contains mechanics ADHD brains genuinely benefit from — and every one of them dies at the same spot: the **recurring maintenance ritual** (weekly review, migration, filing, replanning, board grooming). The ritual is boring, delayed-reward, and executive-function-heavy — the exact task profile ADHD handles worst.
- The predictable arc is the **system-collapse–shame–new-system cycle**: novelty dopamine at setup → the ritual lapses → the system fills with stale, accusing debris → shame → avoidance → a *new* system offers a clean slate plus fresh dopamine. Community shorthand: the **productivity system graveyard**.
- **GTD**'s capture habit, next-action thinking, and "trusted external system" are gold for ADHD (they *are* externalization). Its weekly review is a single point of failure that most ADHDers cannot sustain.
- **Bullet journaling** was invented by Ryder Carroll *because of his ADHD*. Its magic: a blank page absorbs absence without judgment, migration doubles as a natural distributed review, and endless restyling gives sanctioned novelty. Digital clones lose the magic by making rollover frictionless (no intentionality) and gaps visible (guilt). Craft perfectionism is its trap.
- **PARA/Second Brain**: tiny actionability-based taxonomy is smart; per-capture filing decisions and "progressive summarization" are maintenance rituals that quietly die. Capture-first, organize-never (search over sort) fits ADHD better.
- **Manual time blocking** shatters on first slip — plans "collapse by 10am Monday" — and the wreckage triggers calendar anxiety. **Auto-schedulers** (Motion, Reclaim) prove the fix: their most-loved feature among ADHD users is guilt-free automatic *re*-scheduling, not scheduling.
- **Kanban + WIP limits** is the strongest structural fit: visible state offloads working memory, WIP limits externally enforce finishing over starting, and the Done column produces evidence against "I did nothing." Failure mode: the backlog column becomes a shame landfill.
- **Eisenhower matrix** asks users to perform the exact discrimination ADHD impairs (urgent vs. important, felt-urgency vs. real importance). Useful as an assisted lens, not a daily self-serve tool.
- **Tiny daily plans** (MIT, Ivy Lee, 1-3-5) fit ADHD capacity; picking the "most important task" is the hard part, and strict ordering breaks on energy variance.
- **Personal sprints** and the **12 Week Year** exploit short horizons and fresh starts — but self-run ceremonies lapse, and weekly execution scoring converts progress into a grade that triggers the falling-behind spiral.
- Klyr's mandate: build in capture, next actions, WIP limits, visible state, and cyclical fresh starts; automate the review, migration, and re-planning; and make the whole thing survive two weeks of total neglect without accusation.

## How to read this stress test

Each system below is scored on four things: its **core mechanic**, what ADHD brains love, where it breaks against documented ADHD cognition (weak working memory and prospective memory, time blindness, interest-driven motivation, initiation cost, emotional reactivity — see [executive-function](../foundations/executive-function.md), [time-perception](../foundations/time-perception.md), [memory-and-object-permanence](../foundations/memory-and-object-permanence.md), [dopamine-and-motivation](../foundations/dopamine-and-motivation.md)), and what Klyr should steal. Evidence is mostly community and practitioner experience plus clinician commentary; almost none of these systems have been formally trialed in ADHD populations — treat per-system verdicts as strong UX signal, not clinical fact.

## The cross-cutting failure pattern: every system dies at its maintenance ritual

Before the individual autopsies, the shared cause of death. Every classic methodology bundles two things:

1. **Operating mechanics** — capture a thought, move a card, cross off a task. Immediate, small, often satisfying.
2. **A curation ritual** — the GTD weekly review, BuJo monthly migration, PARA filing and distillation, re-planning the shattered calendar, grooming the board, scoring the 12-week plan. Periodic, boring, delayed-payoff, and heavy on exactly the executive functions ADHD taxes most.

The ritual is where trust is manufactured. Skip it and lists drift from reality, the system stops being believable, and — critically — it does not degrade gracefully. It becomes an artifact of failure: overdue flags, untouched pages, a backlog of 400 items. Because ADHD comes with elevated emotional reactivity and often intense sensitivity to evidence of one's own falling-short (see [emotional-regulation-and-rsd](../foundations/emotional-regulation-and-rsd.md)), the stale system doesn't just stop helping — it starts *hurting* to look at. One ADHD engineer describes the two-week arc precisely: a missed day triggers guilt, guilt triggers avoidance, and the tool becomes "a reminder of failure rather than a tool for work" — because systems "punish inconsistency instead of expecting it" [3]. Meanwhile a *new* system offers what the old one can't: setup novelty (a genuinely rewarding project for an interest-driven brain — see [dopamine-and-motivation](../foundations/dopamine-and-motivation.md)) and a slate with no shame residue. Result: the "digital graveyard of abandoned productivity systems" [5], with novelty decay so predictable that ADHD business coaches tell clients to *expect* it as part of the plan [6].

Two corollaries the rest of this doc keeps hitting:

- **People abandon the artifact, not the philosophy.** GTDers still believe in capture; ex-BuJo people still believe in migration. What they flee is the shame-soaked state of a neglected instance. The blank slate is the draw.
- **A system for ADHD must be judged by its neglect behavior**, not its ideal-use behavior. The winning question is not "how well does it work when maintained?" but "what does it look like after two ignored weeks?" (See restart psychology in [habits-and-routines](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md).)

## GTD (Getting Things Done)

**Core mechanic.** David Allen's system: **capture** everything into inboxes; **clarify** each item into a concrete **next action**; organize by project and context; **engage**; and hold it all together with a **weekly review** that keeps the lists matching reality. The prize is a "trusted system" — your head stops holding tasks because something reliable does.

**What ADHD brains love.** GTD is, functionally, Barkley-style externalization two decades before the term reached the mainstream: it moves working memory and prospective memory out of the head (see [memory-and-object-permanence](../foundations/memory-and-object-permanence.md)). ADHD users on the GTD forums single out ubiquitous capture ("being able to dump things into an inbox at any point in time... feels very relieving") and next-action thinking, which converts vague projects into startable steps — directly attacking initiation paralysis (see [task-initiation-and-paralysis](../daily-life/task-initiation-and-paralysis.md)). Context lists adapt naturally into energy-based filters ("deep focus," "brain-dead admin"), which matches ADHD's fluctuating capacity [1].

**Where it breaks.** Two documented places. First, **list bloat**: ADHD optimism about capacity inflates the project list until it becomes aversive — "my GTD lists often get overwhelming to the point that I avoid them" [1]. Second and fatally, the **weekly review as single point of failure**. The entire trust architecture depends on a 30–90 minute, low-stimulation curation session whose cost is immediate and whose payoff is delayed a week — a motivational profile ADHD is nearly guaranteed to lose (temporal discounting; see [time-perception](../foundations/time-perception.md)). Forum members: it "takes so long and so much effort that I rarely finish" [1]. When it lapses, the documented cascade is system decay → decision paralysis → the **Fresh Start Trap**: abandon and rebuild elsewhere [2]. GTD *can* work for ADHD — one GTD trainer with ADHD credits the combination for his career ("there's no way I could have become who I am today... without having both ADHD and GTD") — but survivors describe heavy adaptation: ruthlessly small active lists, a fat Someday/Maybe, review broken into scheduled protected blocks, cherry-picking easy processing on low-energy days [1].

**Steal for Klyr.** Frictionless capture from anywhere; every task stored with a concrete next action (and help generating one); energy/context filters. Then delete the ritual: Klyr itself must perform the review's mechanical work — surfacing stale items one at a time as single keep/kill/someday decisions — instead of asking the user to run an hour of curation on schedule.

## Bullet Journal (BuJo)

**Core mechanic.** A paper notebook plus **rapid logging** (terse bullets for tasks/events/notes), a daily log you start whenever you show up, monthly logs, free-form **collections**, an index, and **migration**: periodically re-writing unfinished tasks forward by hand — or deliberately dropping them.

**What ADHD brains love.** BuJo is the only system on this list *designed by an ADHD brain for itself*: Ryder Carroll built it over years of managing his own ADHD, which he describes as "like trying to catch the rain" [7]. The fits are structural. (a) **A blank page absorbs absence.** Nothing is pre-dated, so a three-week gap is not three weeks of visibly empty boxes — you just start the next daily log. Community guidance is explicit: "forgive yourself and move on" [9]. Contrast dated planners and apps, where absence compounds into documented failure. (b) **Migration is a review that doesn't feel like one.** The hand-copying friction forces a per-task intentionality check — is this worth re-writing? — distributing GTD's weekly review into small, natural moments and giving explicit permission to kill tasks. (c) **Sanctioned novelty.** New spreads, new layouts, a fresh notebook every few months: the redesign itch gets scratched *inside* the system instead of by leaving it. (d) Rapid logging keeps capture cost near zero, and handwriting itself slows and steadies attention [8, 9].

**Where it breaks.** The **craft-perfectionism trap**: Instagram-BuJo culture turns spreads into an art project, setup becomes the hobby, and a messy page becomes a new shame source. Clinician guidance stresses the opposite — "flexibility is the key," pages don't need polish [8]. Migration also has a failure mode: repeatedly re-writing the same task can become ambient guilt rather than decision. And a notebook can't remind you of anything — prospective memory support is zero, which is why hybrid paper+digital setups are common.

**Why digital clones lose the magic.** Digital "migration" is a free *move to tomorrow* click — tasks roll forward forever with zero intentionality cost, accumulating into the overdue pile BuJo was built to prevent. Digital surfaces also reintroduce what paper structurally lacks: red overdue badges, streak-like gap visibility, notification noise. The friction *was* the feature; the forgiveness *was* the interface.

**Steal for Klyr.** Absence tolerance as a core spec (a gap is not an error state; reopening starts a fresh "today," never a debt collection). Migration as a deliberate micro-act: carrying a task forward should be one intentional tap with an equally easy, honorable "let it go" — and Klyr should make repeated carries a gentle decision prompt, not a shame counter. Sanctioned novelty: cosmetic/layout refresh without data loss or rebuild.

## PARA and Building a Second Brain

**Core mechanic.** Tiago Forte's filing scheme: exactly four top-level buckets ordered by **actionability** — **P**rojects (active, defined finish), **A**reas (ongoing responsibilities), **R**esources (interests), **A**rchives (inactive) — plus the CODE loop (Capture, Organize, Distill, Express), with "progressive summarization" of notes over time [10, 11].

**What ADHD brains love.** Four buckets, not forty — a deliberately tiny taxonomy. Sorting by actionability roughly matches the ADHD "now / not-now" time sense. The capture ethos ("your brain is for having ideas, not storing them") is the same externalization win as GTD's.

**Where it breaks.** PARA moves the maintenance tax to the *front* of the pipeline: every capture demands a filing decision, and the project/area/resource boundary is genuinely fuzzy — decision fatigue at exactly the moment capture should be free. Distillation is yet another recurring curation ritual, and in practice the archive becomes a write-only landfill. Organization overhead grows with every capture, while retrieval payoff stays rare — ADHDers seldom revisit notes unprompted (out of sight, out of mind). Even sympathetic guides advise running only PARA-plus-capture and skipping progressive summarization "until it is clearly needed" [11] — i.e., the community itself amputates the ritual.

**Steal for Klyr.** A small, fixed, actionability-ordered structure users never design themselves; capture that files itself later or never (search and machine assistance over manual sorting); **auto-archiving by inactivity** instead of manual filing, with trivial resurrection.

## Time blocking and auto-scheduling (Motion, Reclaim)

**Core mechanic.** Give every task a home on the calendar. Manual time blocking assigns blocks by hand; **auto-schedulers** like Motion and Reclaim place tasks around meetings algorithmically from priority, duration, and deadline — and re-place them when reality intervenes.

**What ADHD brains love.** Blocking makes time *visible and physical* — the calendar shows the collision between what you hope to do and the hours that exist, exposing overcommitment before the day implodes [18]. A pre-decided schedule also answers "what now?", removing a real point of ADHD decision paralysis [13, 14].

**Where manual blocking breaks.** Three compounding failures. (1) **The planning fallacy, amplified.** ADHD time estimation runs on "inconsistent units" (Ari Tuckman); clinical advice is to measure real durations and always allot more time than feels necessary [17]. Optimistic blocks guarantee overruns. (2) **Brittleness.** One 9:30 slip falsifies every block after it. ADHD academic coach Skye Waterson describes perfectly color-coded schedules that "collapsed by 10am on Monday morning" [16]. (3) **The wreckage is emotional.** A day of visibly missed blocks is a shame artifact — calendar anxiety — feeding the avoidance loop (Waterson's "Emotional Wall": avoiding how the task will feel, not the task) [16]. Her salvaged protocol is telling: double every estimate, halve the task list, schedule only peak-energy hours, buffer everything [16].

**What auto-scheduling proves.** The single most-praised feature in ADHD reviews of Motion is one-click **Reschedule All**: when you fall behind, the plan self-heals and you are simply *not behind anymore* — no manual re-planning session, no self-flagellation [12]. Reclaim markets itself on having rescheduled hundreds of millions of conflicts [14] — vendor numbers, but an honest admission that schedules break constantly and the product is really a re-scheduler. The caveats are equally instructive: algorithmic calendars can overpack days toward burnout without a human in the loop [13], feel oppressively structured to users who need low-pressure planning [15], and still depend on disciplined task entry and honest estimates — garbage in, brittle out [12].

**Steal for Klyr.** Plans must self-heal by default: a slipped block quietly reflows; the corpse of the old plan is never left on display. Estimates treated as elastic and corrected from observed actuals. Overbooking friction at plan time, not guilt at failure time. And schedule-as-suggestion, never contract — with visible reasoning and easy override, because ADHD users distrust a black box moving their day.

## Kanban and WIP limits

**Core mechanic.** Columns as workflow states (e.g., Backlog / Today / Doing / Done), one card per task, and a **work-in-progress (WIP) limit** capping how many cards may sit in Doing at once.

**What ADHD brains love.** This is the strongest structural fit on the list. A board makes state *ambient*: "instead of asking your working memory to track state, the layout holds the state" [18] — working memory comfortably juggles only a handful of items, and the board carries the rest [18, 19]. It solves out-of-sight-out-of-mind by keeping work literally in sight. The WIP limit is an external, mechanical brake on the signature ADHD pattern of starting everything and finishing nothing: capping Doing at 2–3 cards forces an honest finish-or-park choice before anything new starts [18, 19]. Moving a card is a micro-reward; the Done column accumulates *evidence* against the end-of-day feeling of "I did nothing."

**Where it breaks.** The backlog column becomes the new landfill — hundreds of stale cards turning the board into a wall of shame; boards still assume a grooming ritual (clear Done, re-stock Today) that lapses like every other ritual; oversized vague cards stall in Doing ("when a board gets chaotic, the task breakdown is too coarse" [19]); and digital boards that scroll are boards that hide — anything off-screen is gone again. Community setup advice converges: 3–4 columns max, whole board visible at a glance, Today capped around 3 [19].

**Steal for Klyr.** WIP limits as a first-class, on-by-default mechanic (gentle enforcement: to start a fourth thing, park one). Visible state as the primary UI metaphor. Backlog *reachable but not resident* in the daily view. Auto-grooming: Done sweeps itself, stale cards sink and fade instead of glaring.

## The Eisenhower matrix

**Core mechanic.** Sort tasks on two axes — urgent/not, important/not — then do, schedule, delegate, or delete by quadrant.

**Stress-test result: the matrix demands the exact discrimination ADHD impairs.** Clinician Sharon Saline: prioritization is an executive function, and many ADHDers experience *all* tasks as equally critical until a genuine crisis forces the ordering — with emotion, not valuation, driving what feels significant [20]. Under stress the felt-urgency signal degrades further: "difficulty distinguishing what's truly urgent from what simply feels urgent," and defaulting "to whatever feels loudest or most emotionally charged in the moment" [21]. Temporal discounting adds a systematic bias: Quadrant 2 (important, not urgent) is motivationally invisible until procrastination promotes everything into Quadrant 1, where deadline panic finally supplies activation — so the matrix degenerates into "everything urgent, ever." "Delegate" is also frequently unavailable in personal life. Clinical orgs still endorse the matrix as a *supported* exercise — a worksheet done with a coach, colors, small task chunks [21, 22] — which is precisely the point: it works as an occasionally assisted lens, not a self-serve daily mechanic.

**Steal for Klyr.** Never present users a blank 2x2 and ask for cold classification. Infer urgency from deadlines and dates; elicit importance through lightweight, well-timed prompts ("which of these matters most to future-you?"); and actively protect important-non-urgent work by converting it into concrete, scheduled, small next actions — because it will never win a salience contest on its own.

## MIT, Ivy Lee, and 1-3-5: tiny daily plans

**Core mechanic.** Plan tomorrow the night before, and cap it hard. **Ivy Lee**: six tasks, ranked, worked strictly in order, leftovers roll forward [23]. **MIT**: name 1–3 Most Important Tasks. **1-3-5**: one big, three medium, five small.

**What ADHD brains love.** Near-zero maintenance (the whole system is one small daily act); a hard cap that externally fights chronic overcommitment; and pre-deciding the morning's first move the night before — separating cool-headed choosing from hot-state doing, which removes the "what do I even start with" gap where mornings die. ADHD-community adaptations of the pattern lean on the small-wins momentum of finishing the little items [24].

**Where it breaks.** Picking the MIT *is* the impaired skill (see Eisenhower above) — the cap doesn't help if the selection step stalls. Strict ordering collides with energy variance: if task #1 is emotionally blocked, an in-order list halts entirely. Rollover quietly rebuilds a backlog one leftover at a time. And even this minimal system hangs on an end-of-day ritual that depends on ending the day at a planned time — not an ADHD strength.

**Steal for Klyr.** A daily plan capped by default (1–3 focus items), assembled with Klyr doing the pre-filtering (deadline pressure, staleness, user-marked importance) so choosing is a confirmation, not an open decision. Order as suggestion, never lock. Leftovers migrate BuJo-style — deliberately, without visible debt accumulation.

## Personal sprints / agile-for-one

**Core mechanic.** Scrum scaled to one person: a backlog, a 1–2 week **sprint** with committed scope, daily check-in, and a **retrospective** before the next sprint starts.

**What ADHD brains love.** Sprints institutionalize the fresh start: every cycle ends, closes its books, and begins clean — a structural, shame-free restart rhythm (the thing ADHDers otherwise get only by abandoning systems; see [habits-and-routines](../daily-life/habits-and-routines.md)). Timeboxes create the near deadlines ADHD motivation actually responds to. Retros reframe review as process improvement rather than self-judgment, and tracking what actually got done teaches honest capacity over time. Practitioners with ADHD report real traction — one Scrum professional runs a nightly personal scrum — while admitting the retro is the first ceremony to slip [25].

**Where it breaks.** Every ceremony is a self-run ritual with no team gravity to keep it happening; solo "commitment" has no external accountability; planning overhead can exceed the work for life-sized tasks; and velocity/commitment metrics can curdle into another grade to fail.

**Steal for Klyr.** Cycles that close *themselves*: the week (or sprint) auto-ends, auto-archives, and auto-opens clean. A blameless, auto-generated recap ("here's what happened, here's what carried") replacing the self-run retro. Capacity learning from actuals feeding next cycle's suggested load.

## The 12 Week Year

**Core mechanic.** Treat 12 weeks as a full "year": 1–3 goals, weekly tactic lists, weekly scoring of your execution percentage, and review [26].

**What ADHD brains love.** The horizon compression is genuinely ADHD-compatible: 12 weeks is close enough to feel real where annual goals evaporate — "goals set in January are forgotten by March" [27]. Urgency without a year of abstraction.

**Where it breaks.** ADHD coach Caren Magill's assessment: the system fixates on outcomes over process, leaves no room to iterate strategies, and — decisive for this population — compressed timelines *accelerate* the falling-behind spiral: "when you have ADHD, very often you already feel like you're falling behind," and the pressure ends in abandoning the goal [26]. Weekly execution scoring converts effort into a grade; a bad score is a streak-break with a percentage attached (see [motivation-and-gamification](./motivation-and-gamification.md) on why scores backfire).

**Steal for Klyr.** Short goal horizons (weeks, not years) as the default planning vocabulary; progress framing ("what moved") with no execution percentages; goal timelines that stretch without ceremony or judgment — "sometimes a goal needs to take as long as it needs to take" [26].

## What survives the stress test

| System | Mechanic Klyr keeps | Ritual Klyr automates away or bans |
|---|---|---|
| GTD | Ubiquitous capture; next actions; energy/context filters | Weekly review → continuous micro-review, one decision at a time |
| Bullet Journal | Migration-as-review; absence tolerance; sanctioned novelty | Manual re-copying; craft perfectionism; frictionless auto-rollover (ban) |
| PARA / Second Brain | Tiny fixed actionability taxonomy; capture-first ethos | Per-capture filing; progressive summarization → auto-archive by inactivity |
| Time blocking / Motion, Reclaim | Time made visible; pre-decided "what now"; overbooking collision | Manual re-planning after slips → self-healing schedule; plan-wreckage display (ban) |
| Kanban | WIP limits; ambient visible state; Done as evidence | Board grooming → auto-sweep; backlog-in-face (ban) |
| Eisenhower | Urgency inference + importance elicitation; Q2 protection | Cold self-classification into quadrants (ban as a user task) |
| MIT / Ivy Lee / 1-3-5 | Hard-capped daily plan; night-before choosing | Strict serial order; unassisted MIT selection |
| Personal sprints | Cyclical fresh starts; capacity learning; blameless recap | Self-run ceremonies → auto-closing cycles |
| 12 Week Year | Short goal horizons | Weekly execution scoring (ban) |

## Design implications for Klyr

1. **Klyr must be the system that survives two weeks of neglect.** Reopening after any absence shows a calm "welcome back — here's what matters now" state: no overdue pile, no red badge wall, no gap visualization. This is the single highest-leverage differentiator, because every incumbent system's neglect state is what kills it [1, 2, 3, 5].
2. **Automate the review; never schedule one.** Klyr performs the mechanical work of GTD's weekly review continuously — surfacing one stale item at a time as a single keep/kill/someday tap at natural moments (e.g., during daily planning). An hour-long curation ritual as a prerequisite for system trust is a designed-in death [1, 2].
3. **Make carrying a task forward a deliberate micro-act, and letting it go equally honorable.** Adopt BuJo migration: unfinished tasks don't auto-roll silently (that builds the pile) and don't display overdue-day counters (that builds shame); after repeated carries, Klyr asks the intentionality question — "still worth your attention?" — with kill/someday one tap away [7, 8, 9].
4. **Capture must be instant and decision-free.** No filing, no project assignment, no quadrant at capture time; organization happens later via suggestion, search, and auto-archiving by inactivity. PARA shows front-loaded filing decisions tax capture exactly when it must be free [1, 10, 11].
5. **Every stored task should carry a concrete next action.** GTD's most validated mechanic for ADHD: startability is stored with the task, not reconstructed at initiation time. Klyr should help generate the next action when the user captures something vague [1].
6. **WIP limits on by default.** A small active set (2–3 in "doing," ~1–3 in today's plan); starting beyond the cap prompts an honest finish-or-park choice. This externally enforces the finishing that ADHD self-regulation can't, and it's the anti-overcommitment mechanic with the strongest community verdict [18, 19].
7. **Plans self-heal by default.** When a block or a day slips, Klyr reflows the plan automatically (with visible reasoning and undo); the user is never shown the wreckage of the old plan. Motion's ADHD reviews prove guilt-free rescheduling — not scheduling — is the loved feature [12, 16].
8. **Correct the planning fallacy for the user.** Learn real durations from actuals, pad estimates by default, and surface the collision between planned work and available hours *at plan time*. Never let honest optimism set the user up for a visibly failed day [16, 17, 18].
9. **Never ask for cold urgent/important classification.** Infer urgency from dates; elicit importance with tiny well-timed prompts; actively protect important-non-urgent tasks by making them small, concrete, and scheduled — they will never win on salience alone [20, 21].
10. **Build structural fresh starts.** Days and weeks auto-close, auto-archive, and open clean, with an auto-generated blameless recap ("what moved, what carried") replacing self-run retros and reviews. This gives the restart ADHDers currently get only by abandoning systems [3, 25, 26].
11. **Sanctioned novelty, bounded.** Let users restyle and re-layout without data loss or rebuild, so the new-system itch is scratched inside Klyr — but keep customization shallow, because complexity creep is itself a documented abandonment trigger. Tension to test: novelty extends engagement, yet every added option raises maintenance load [5, 6, 7].
12. **No execution scores, no streaks, no grades.** Progress evidence (a Done record, "what moved this week") — yes; percentages, streak counters, and completion grades — no. Scoring converts fluctuation into failure for a population with elevated shame reactivity [4, 5, 26]; see [motivation-and-gamification](./motivation-and-gamification.md).
13. **Backlog exists, but out of sight.** Someday/captured items must be reliably reachable (trust requires nothing is lost) yet never squat in the daily view; stale items sink and fade rather than glare. The board that shows everything becomes a shame wall [18, 19].
14. **Automation must stay legible.** GTD's lesson is that the system only works when *trusted*; auto-healing, auto-archiving, and auto-planning therefore need visible reasoning, notification restraint, and one-tap undo — otherwise Klyr becomes a black box moving people's lives, which ADHD users demonstrably resent in Motion [1, 13, 15].

## Open questions

- **Frictionless migration paradox.** BuJo's review power comes from hand-copy friction; Klyr wants low friction everywhere. Is a one-tap "carry forward" intentional *enough*, or does it decay into auto-rollover? Needs A/B testing with real ADHD users (e.g., tap-to-carry vs. carry-with-question after N carries).
- **Auto-kill trust boundary.** Will users experience automatic fading/archiving of stale tasks as relief or as data loss that breaks system trust? Where is the consent line (suggest-then-archive vs. archive-then-notify)?
- **Optimal default WIP and daily caps.** Community folklore says 2–3; no controlled evidence exists for ADHD populations. Test defaults and whether users should be allowed to raise them.
- **Does self-healing scheduling reduce abandonment?** Motion/Reclaim publish no ADHD retention data; the hypothesis that guilt-free rescheduling extends system lifespan is plausible but unproven.
- **Novelty dosing.** How much restyling/refresh actually extends retention before it becomes displacement activity (system fiddling instead of doing)? Can Klyr detect a setup-spiral and gently redirect?
- **Paper hybrid.** Many ADHDers keep paper for capture/planning feel and digital for reminders. Should Klyr embrace a hybrid workflow (e.g., photo-import of paper plans) rather than compete with paper?
